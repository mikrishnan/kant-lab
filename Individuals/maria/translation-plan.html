Kant Lab · Executive summary · October 2026

An English Kant you can always see through to the German

How to build a consistent, scholarly-conventional English layer over the whole corpus in this repository — Meier, Baumgarten, the Logik transcripts, the Reflexionen — so readers get fluent English while every key term stays one hover away from its German original, and so it costs tokens once rather than every time.

The short version

  1. Make the glossary the product, not the translation. A hand-curated termbase of ~200–300 Kantian terms with fixed, conventional English renderings (Erkenntnis → cognition, Vorstellung → representation), their Latin equivalents and their period spellings is what makes a machine translation consistent and citable. Everything else is replaceable; the termbase is the scholarly asset.
  2. Do everything you can without a language model. Cutting the text into sentences, normalising 18th-century spelling, finding termbase terms, and checking the result are all deterministic scripts that cost nothing and can be re-run forever.
  3. Spend tokens in exactly one place, exactly once. The model translates each sentence a single time, sees only the termbase entries that sentence actually contains, returns structured output already aligned to the German, and the result is cached against a hash of the German. Change one glossary entry and you re-translate only the sentences that contain that term.
  4. Ship static files; the reader never calls an API. That fits how this repository already works (no server, no build, one HTML file per tool) and means reading costs zero tokens.
  5. Show the German at the level of the term, not only the sentence. In the reader, every termbase term in the English is a quiet link back to the exact German word on the page — its period spelling, its lemma, its Latin, the glossary note, and every other place it occurs.

IWhat the prototypes already taught us

Over the last weeks we translated Busolt, Blomberg and Meier into English, aligned Meier sentence by sentence, broke its sentences into glossed phrases, and wired a hover translation into the reader. That was the right way to find out what matters. It is not yet the right way to translate the corpus.

What worked

  • Keying everything to the source's own IDs and character offsets. The German never gets retyped or "cleaned"; the reader cuts it out of data/ itself.
  • Mechanical checks that can't be argued with. "The German phrases concatenated reproduce the sentence exactly" was verified for all 1,697 Meier units; editorial brackets [AA: …] were checked on every block.
  • Translating at the sentence, glossing at the phrase. Readers liked a phrase-level hover; it is the natural unit for cross-referencing.
  • Parallel, batched work. ~1.1 M characters were translated in under two hours.

What we'd change

  • No shared glossary. 29 independent agents each got a short list of renderings and improvised the rest, so the same German term can come out differently in different chunks.
  • Wrong unit. Logik blocks are sometimes page fragments, so we translated half-sentences. Sentences should be rebuilt before translation.
  • Alignment after the fact. We had to infer which English sentence matched which German one (559 of 563 §§ matched cleanly, the rest needed merging). If the translator returns English keyed to German sentence IDs, alignment is free and exact.
  • Glosses that disagree with the sentence. The phrase pass ran separately from the sentence pass, so a gloss can contradict its own sentence (wenn man nach und nach → "that one one after another"). One pass should produce both.
  • Expensive per character. Interactive agents spend most of their tokens reading files and calling tools — roughly 2.2 M tokens for 1.1 M characters. A batch pipeline sends only text.

The existing files (translations/*-en.json, meier-aligned, meier-phrases) stay useful as a baseline: once the pipeline exists, we can measure how much more consistent its output is than the prototype's.

IIThe architecture: one model step, everything else a script

FREE1 · SegmentRebuild sentences from data/ blocks; give each a stable ID and its character offsets into the source. Round-trip check.
FREE2 · NormaliseMap period spellings to a modern lemma — Urtheil → Urteil, Erkenntniß → Erkenntnis, seyn → sein — for matching only. The display keeps the original.
FREE3 · Tag termsFind every termbase term in every sentence, with offsets. Pick each term's sense where the termbase says how.
TOKENS4 · TranslatePer batch of sentences: style guide (cached) + only the glossary entries present + the sentences. Returns JSON keyed by sentence ID, with term and phrase alignment.
FREE5 · ValidateCoverage, brackets, § and page refs, numbers, required term renderings, length outliers. Prints a report ending in clean.
FREE6 · PublishStatic .js files beside the data, loaded by <script src> so they work from file:// and on Pages.

deterministic, re-runnable, zero tokens the only step that calls a model

This is the same shape as the repository's existing extractors (parse_refl.py, parse_logik.py): a script that reads data/, writes generated files that are never hand-edited, and prints a report you read before you trust the output. Human corrections go in a separate overrides file keyed by sentence ID and German hash, so a regeneration never wipes them.

IIIThe termbase is the heart of it

Consistency doesn't come from a better prompt. It comes from deciding, once and in writing, how each term is rendered, and then having a script check that the translation obeyed.

One entry per sense

Achtung is "respect" in the moral writings, but Achtung geben is "pay attention" — the second appears in our own Meier phrase data. Satz is "proposition" in logic and "sentence" elsewhere. Erklärung in Meier often means "definition". Entries split by sense and say how to tell them apart.

Every period spelling

The transcripts spell freely: Erkenntniß, Erkentniß, Erkenntniss; Urtheil; Vernunftschlüße, Vernunft Schlüße; Definizion; Saz, Säzze; Logic; Qvelle. Rules (th→t, ß↔ss, z↔tz, c↔k) cover most of them; each entry lists the exceptions.

Compounds and Latin

Vernunfterkenntnis, Verbindungsbegriff, Mittelbegriff need entries of their own. The texts gloss themselves in Latin (Urteil (iudicium)), so each entry carries its Latin side too. That side also serves Baumgarten's Latin Metaphysica.

The hash is what saves money. If the German hash and the termbase version of every term in the sentence are unchanged, the stored translation is still valid and nobody pays to redo it.

Where the savings come from

  • Batch API: half price for work that can wait (results within about a day). Translation can always wait.
  • Prompt caching: the style guide and fixed instructions are identical on every request, so they're read from cache at a small fraction of the input price.
  • Glossary slicing: each batch sees the ~20 termbase entries it contains, not all 300.
  • Hash cache and targeted re-runs: change the rendering of Fürwahrhalten and only the few hundred sentences containing it are re-translated.
  • Structured output in one pass: sentence, term and phrase alignment all come back together, so there's no separate alignment or gloss run.
  • Zero tokens at reading time: the reader loads static files.

Prices from Anthropic's published list as of 6 October 2026: Claude Opus 5.5 $4 / $20 per million input / output tokens; Claude Sonnet 5.5 $2 / $10. The range above allows for JSON and alignment overhead and some thinking. The pilot should measure the real figure before the full run; Sonnet 5.5 would roughly halve it, and is worth testing on the pilot against Opus 5.5 for quality. Check current pricing before committing.

VIIQuality, and being honest about it

The repository has a hard rule: anything a tool worked out for itself must be visibly marked, and anything the Academy Edition says must survive. A machine translation is the clearest possible case of "worked out for itself". So:

Checked by script, every run

  • Every sentence has English; nothing silently dropped.
  • Editorial brackets, § references, AA page numbers and numerals carried through exactly.
  • Every tagged term rendered as the termbase says, or flagged as a reasoned deviation.
  • Phrase alignment covers the German exactly (the check we already run on Meier).
  • Length-ratio outliers listed for a person to look at.

Checked by people, gradually

  • A review queue per text: machine → checked → revised, shown on every sentence.
  • A small random sample graded each run (a second model can pre-screen, but a person signs off).
  • Corrections in an overrides file that survives regeneration; recurring corrections become termbase changes.
  • A decisions log for disputed renderings, with references.
What this cannot promise: literary quality, or correctness in garbled passages of the transcripts. It aims at a consistent, terminologically disciplined working translation, explicitly labelled as such, that sends the reader to the German at every term that matters. Published translations (Cambridge and others) stay under copyright: we take their terminology decisions as reference, not their sentences.

IXDecisions that are yours to make

Which convention? I'd propose the Cambridge Edition's renderings as the default, with deviations recorded. The alternative is a house style of our own, which means more decisions and less recognisability for readers who know the published translations.
Where does it live? Pilot it in Individuals/maria/. Once it works, the termbase, segments and translations belong in data/, since every tool could use them, and data/ is co-owned by you and Sophia. The pipeline commands would then go into the root CLAUDE.md, as the repository's rules require for extractors.
Who decides the termbase? It needs a person who reads Kant in German. A few focused sessions, perhaps split between you and Sophia by text, are enough for v1.
What happens to the prototype translations? Keep them as the baseline to measure against, and retire them from the reader once the pipeline's output replaces them.
Which model? Claude Opus 5.5 by default for the translation step, with Sonnet 5.5 tested on the pilot. Whether a cheaper model is good enough is a quality call you should make from the pilot's output, not something to assume.
