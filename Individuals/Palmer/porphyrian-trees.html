Arbor Porphyriana · A Reception History

The Tree of Porphyry

The Porphyrian division of substance and the problem of universals — a critical survey of its reception from late antiquity to formal ontology

Late Antique Early Medieval High Medieval Late Medieval Renaissance Early Modern Modern

The Tree of Porphyry is not, strictly, Porphyry's. The Isagoge (c. 268 AD), his introduction to Aristotle's Categories, sets out the five predicables (genus, species, differentia, proprium, accidens) and, in discussing genus and species, runs a single chain of division from substance through body, animate body, animal and rational animal to man and the individuals Socrates and Plato. It is a chain of dichotomous divisions by differentiae, not a diagram. The branching figure familiar from the manuscripts is a later, medieval pedagogical device, and the term arbor porphyriana is later still.

What the tree carries philosophically is the question Porphyry himself declines to answer at the opening of the Isagoge — whether genera and species subsist or consist in bare thoughts alone, and, if they subsist, whether they are bodies or incorporeal, and whether they are separated from sensibles or in them. Each entry below is read against that question, and against the tree's second career as a formal structure (a taxonomy, a lattice, a class hierarchy). Entries give primary sources first; the numbered notes at the end flag editions and points of scholarly dispute.

Late Antique & Patristic Period, 3rd – 6th century

Porphyry of Tyre c. 234 – c. 305 AD

Isagoge (Gk. Eisagōgē), Introduction to Aristotle's Categories

The source text. The division of substance into body, animate body, animal, rational animal, man and individuals is given as a worked example of genus and species. The tree is generated by differentiae (corporeal/incorporeal, animate/inanimate, sensitive/insensitive, rational/irrational, mortal/immortal), with Socrates and Plato as individuals, not species. The prefatory aporiai on the ontological status of universals are raised and expressly set aside, which is what made the text so productive for later commentators. Porphyry also locates the tree within Aristotle's Categories (substance as genus generalissimum), though how far the Isagoge is itself committed to an Aristotelian reading is disputed.

Ed. A. Busse, Porphyrii Isagoge, CAG IV.1 (1887). Tr. J. Barnes, Porphyry: Introduction (Oxford, 2003), with commentary. Cf. Aristotle, Cat. 2–5; Top. I.4–5, VI.

Boethius c. 477 – 524 AD

In Isagogen Porphyrii commentorum editio prima et secunda; In Categorias Aristotelis libri quattuor; transl. of the Isagoge (Latin)

The Latin channel. Boethius's translation of the Isagoge and his two commentaries fixed the Latin vocabulary (genus generalissimum, genus subalternum, species specialissima, individuum) and framed the problem of universals for the medieval West. In the second commentary he takes up Porphyry's aporiai and offers a broadly Alexandrian solution: genera and species exist in singulars as sensible and are understood as universal. How far this settles the question, or merely reports Alexander's and Porphyry's options, is contested (see note 2).

Ed. S. Brandt, Boethii in Isagogen Porphyrii commenta, CSEL 48 (1906). Tr. (second commentary, I.10–11) in Spade, Five Texts on the Mediaeval Problem of Universals (1994). Lit. Gracia & Newton, "Medieval Theories of the Categories", SEP.

Early Medieval Period, 8th – 10th century

Alcuin of York c. 735 – 804

Dialectica (dialogue with Charlemagne); cf. the Pseudo-Augustinian Categoriae decem

Alcuin offers a compendious account of the predicables for the Carolingian court. Its sources are Boethius, Isidore and the pseudo-Augustinian Categoriae decem rather than Porphyry directly: the Dialectica belongs to the transmission of the tree as a school schema, not to philosophical discussion of it. It is useful chiefly as evidence for what of the Boethian apparatus was available before the twelfth century.

Ed. PL 101, 949–976. Lit. Marenbon, From the Circle of Alcuin to the School of Auxerre (Cambridge, 1981).

Carolingian Pedagogy Transmission
John Scottus Eriugena c. 815 – c. 877

Periphyseon (De divisione naturae), Bk I

Periphyseon I opens with a fourfold division of natura (creating and uncreated; created and creating; created and not creating; neither). Eriugena explicitly deploys the Boethian apparatus of division into genera and species (Per. I.1–3), and treats the Aristotelian categories as applicable only to creatures, not to God. The relation to the Porphyrian tree is one of method (diairesis and analysis) rather than content, and the resemblance should not be overstated.

Ed. E. Jeauneau, CCCM 161–165 (1996–2003). Lit. Moran, The Philosophy of John Scottus Eriugena (Cambridge, 1989).

High Scholastic Period, 11th – 13th century

Peter Abelard 1079 – 1142

Logica 'Ingredientibus' (glosses on the Isagoge); Logica 'Nostrorum petitioni'; Dialectica

Abelard rejects the realist options in the Ingredientibus glosses: no universal is a res, and neither is it a bare vox (a physical utterance). What is predicated of many is a sermo, a word as significant, whose signification rests on a common cause of imposition (the status) in things and on an understanding of the thing that abstracts from its particulars. The label "nominalist" is conventional but contested, and Abelard's own target is Porphyry's three aporiai. His treatment of the tree thus anticipates the later "conceptualist" reading more than Ockham's.

Ed. B. Geyer, Peter Abaelards philosophische Schriften, BGPhMA 21 (1919–27); Logica Ingredientibus: Glossae super Porphyrium. Tr. Spade (1994). Lit. King, Peter Abailard and the Problem of Universals (diss., Princeton, 1982); Tweedale, Abailard on Universals (1976).

Albert the Great (Albertus Magnus) c. 1200 – 1280

Liber de praedicabilibus (Super Porphyrium de V universalibus)

Albert's paraphrase of the Isagoge sets out the Avicennian doctrine of the threefold being of the universal (ante rem, in re, post rem), which becomes the standard scholastic way of reconciling the tree with Aristotle. The nature considered in itself is neither universal nor singular (Avicenna's "equinity is only equinity"). The universal as such belongs to the intellect, but its basis is in things (fundamentum in re). The tree thereby receives a metaphysical ground without a Platonic one.

Ed. Borgnet, Opera omnia I (Paris, 1890); crit. ed. Cologne Opera omnia, I.1. Source Avicenna, Metaphysica V.1–2 (Lat.). Lit. de Libera, La querelle des universaux (1996).

Dominican Avicennian essence Realism
Thomas Aquinas 1225 – 1274

De ente et essentia c. 2–3; Summa theologiae I q. 3 a. 5, q. 85 a. 3; In Boethii De trinitate q. 5 a. 3

Aquinas does not comment on the Isagoge. (A commentary sometimes printed with his works is spurious.) He treats the tree where it bears on his own questions. In De ente c. 2 the Porphyrian ordering of substance (body, animal, man) is used to analyse how a nature signifies as part and as whole, and why an essentia is predicable of an individuum only when taken ut totum. In ST I q. 3 a. 5 God is placed outside every genus, which blocks the tree at its top. In I q. 85 a. 3 he treats the order in which the intellect forms concepts, from the more to the less general. His position on universals is a moderate realism following Avicenna and Albert.

Ed. Leonine, Opera omnia vols. 4–12, 22, 50. Lit. Owens, "Common Nature", Medieval Studies 19 (1957); Pasnau, Metaphysical Themes 1274–1671 (2011).

Late Medieval Period, 13th – 15th century

William of Ockham c. 1287 – 1347

Expositio in librum Porphyrii de Praedicabilibus; Summa logicae I cc. 14–25; Ordinatio I d. 2 qq. 4–8

Ockham reads the tree as a structure of concepts and terms, not things. Only singulars exist, and a universal is an intention of the soul (a conceptus, or on his early view a fictum) that is predicable of many by signification. SL I c. 14 and following attack the realist positions of Scotus and others in detail. Genus and species are second intentions, and their order is an order of predication among terms. The tree remains in use as logic; its claim to reflect a real order of common natures is what is refused.

Ed. Opera philosophica (St. Bonaventure: I, SL, 1974; II, Expositio, 1978); Opera theologica II (Ordinatio I d. 2). Tr. Loux, Ockham's Theory of Terms (1974). Lit. Adams, William Ockham (1987), chs. 1–4.

Peter of Spain (Pope John XXI) fl. mid-13th century (identity disputed)

Tractatus (Summulae logicales), Tr. I–II

The Tractatus was the standard introductory logic text of the later Middle Ages and the Renaissance. Tractatus I introduces the basic notions, and Tractatus II (De praedicabilibus) sets out the five predicables for students at the arts faculty. Authorship is contested: the traditional identification with Pope John XXI (d. 1277) is now doubted, and some scholars propose a Dominican or a different Petrus Hispanus. The work's importance for the tree is institutional, as the form in which most students met it.

Ed. L. M. de Rijk, Tractatus called afterwards Summule logicales (Assen, 1972). Lit. Ashworth, "Peter of Spain" (SEP).

Renaissance & Humanist Period, 15th – 16th century

Rudolf Agricola 1444 – 1485

De inventione dialectica libri tres (written 1470s; printed 1515)

Agricola reorients dialectic from the theory of the syllogism toward the discovery and arrangement of arguments through the loci. The Porphyrian predicables are retained but now as a set of "places" for finding middle terms (loci of genus, species, differentia, proprium, accident), not as a map of being. Agricola's book is a main source for the sixteenth-century transformation of logic, Ramus included. The claim that he is "anti-Porphyrian" is an overstatement.

Ed. L. Mundt, De inventione dialectica libri tres (Tübingen, 1992), with German tr. Lit. Mack, Renaissance Argument (Leiden, 1993).

Humanism Topics Invention
Petrus Ramus (Pierre de la Ramée) 1515 – 1572

Dialecticae partitiones (1543); Dialecticae institutiones (1543); Aristotelicae animadversiones (1543); Dialectique (1555)

Ramus ties dialectic to method as a descent from the most general to the particular by successive dichotomies. The resulting printed "Ramist tables" are the nearest early-modern descendants of the visual arbor. Ramus does not simply continue the Porphyrian structure: dichotomous bifurcation of definitions is substituted for division by essential differentiae, and the realist content is largely dropped. Walter Ong is the standard (and polemical) account of how the tables shaped print culture; Ong's picture of a shift to "spatialised" thought is itself debated.

Lit. W. J. Ong, Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue (Cambridge, MA, 1958); Sharratt (ed.), Peter Ramus: Dialectique 1555; Meerhoff, Rhétorique et poétique (1986).

Early Modern Period, 17th – 18th century

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz 1646 – 1716

Dissertatio de arte combinatoria (1666); Generales inquisitiones de analysi notionum et veritatum (1686); Nouveaux essais III

Leibniz generalises the logic of genus and differentia into an analysis of concepts into simple ones, with a combinatorial calculus. In the Dissertatio he treats a concept as a conjunction of simpler concepts, and approvingly cites the Porphyrian ordering as a model of a scientia generalis. The Generales inquisitiones give the first mature algebra of concepts (with identity and inclusion in), later read as an anticipation of Boolean algebra by Couturat, Lewis and Kneale. How far he commits to a finite alphabet of simple concepts is a famous interpretive question. In Nouveaux essais III he criticises the realist reading of species and treats kinds as nominal essences, except for the real essence knowable only to God.

Ed. A VI.1 (Dissertatio), A VI.4 (Generales inquisitiones); GP V (NE). Lit. Couturat, La logique de Leibniz (1901); Lenzen, "Leibniz's Logic" in Handbook of the History of Logic III (2004); Mates, The Philosophy of Leibniz (1986).

Antoine Arnauld & Pierre Nicole 1612–1694 & 1625–1695

La Logique, ou l'art de penser (1662), Part I

The Logic follows Descartes in treating ideas as the primary units of logic, and reduces the Porphyrian apparatus to a theory of the extension and comprehension of ideas. Part I treats universal ideas and the five predicables briefly and with some impatience, as scholastic matter retained for its usefulness in teaching. This pairing of extension and comprehension is the route by which a good deal of the tree's content reaches Kant and the German school logics.

Ed. P. Clair & F. Girbal (Paris, 1965). Tr. J. V. Buroker (Cambridge, 1996). Lit. Buroker, "Port-Royal Logic" (SEP).

Immanuel Kant 1724 – 1804

Kritik der reinen Vernunft, Appendix to the Transcendental Dialectic (A642–668/B670–696); Logik (Jäsche), §§ 7–11; Meier, Auszug aus der Vernunftlehre

Kant takes the tree over from the Wolffian school logic (Meier's Auszug, which he lectured from) and transposes it. In the Appendix the ascent to higher genera and descent to lower species are no longer a map of being but regulative logical principles of reason: homogeneity (the unity of genera), specification (the manifold of species) and continuity (their affinity). Reason may presuppose the systematic order as a maxim of inquiry, but may not assert it of things in themselves. Kant also holds that in the use of concepts there is no lowest species (Logik § 11), the tree being open downward. The scholastic tree is thereby transformed into a regulative idea.

Ed. AA 3 and 4 (KrV, B and A); AA 9 (Logik); AA 16 (Reflexionen on Meier's Auszug). Lit. Grier, Kant's Doctrine of Transcendental Illusion (2001); Allison, Kant's Transcendental Idealism (2004).

Modern & Contemporary Period, 19th – 21st century

W. V. O. Quine 1908 – 2000

"Natural Kinds" (1969), in Ontological Relativity and Other Essays; Word and Object (1960); "On What There Is" (1948)

Quine does not discuss the tree, but "Natural Kinds" treats its descendant problem: what licenses the classification of things into kinds that support induction. He ties kinds to a notion of similarity that is innate and, by natural selection, roughly tracks the world. The notion becomes dispensable as science matures and replaces "kind" talk with theoretical identities (with the exception of its use as a prescientific tool). This is a deflationary account of what the Aristotelian tradition took as essence, in a naturalistic idiom. The relation to Ockham on universals is a matter of nominalism about abstracta, and Quine's reasons are different (ontological commitment through quantification).

Lit. Hacking, "A Tradition of Natural Kinds", Phil. Studies 61 (1991); Bird & Tobin, "Natural Kinds", SEP; Kripke, Naming and Necessity (1980) and Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975) for the essentialist reply.

OWL / Description Logics community 1980s – present

Brachman, "What IS-A Is and Isn't" (1983); W3C OWL (2004), OWL 2 (2009); The Description Logic Handbook (2003)

The tree's formal descendant is the IS-A taxonomy of knowledge representation. In description logics, subClassOf (subsumption) corresponds to species under genus, and a defined class (necessary and sufficient conditions) corresponds roughly to genus + differentia, whereas a primitive class corresponds to a species whose differentia is not given. The correspondence has limits: the tree is a strict single-inheritance hierarchy with essential differentiae, while OWL permits multiple inheritance and open-world reasoning, and its treatment of individuals is closer to instances than to individua. Ontology engineering (e.g. BFO, DOLCE) revives the question of the tree's metaphysical commitments.

Lit. Baader et al. (eds.), The Description Logic Handbook (Cambridge, 2003); Smith, "Ontology" in Floridi (ed.), Blackwell Guide to the Philosophy of Computing and Information (2004); Arp, Smith & Spear, Building Ontologies with BFO (2015).

Notes

  1. Dating. The Isagoge is conventionally dated to c. 268 AD, after Porphyry's period with Plotinus. Bibliographic dates for the medieval authors are approximate.
  2. Boethius on universals. The second commentary presents a solution then immediately flags its debt to Alexander of Aphrodisias. Whether Boethius endorses it is debated. Compare Spade (1994) with Marenbon, Boethius (2003).
  3. The figure. The earliest surviving branching diagrams of the tree are found in manuscripts of the Boethian commentaries and in late-medieval logic textbooks, and the label arbor porphyriana is not ancient.
  4. Scope. This catalogue is selective. Notable omissions include Avicenna, Duns Scotus (on the natura communis and the formal distinction), Suárez (Disputationes metaphysicae VI–VII), Wolff (Philosophia rationalis sive logica), and Hegel (Wissenschaft der Logik, on Gattung and Art).
