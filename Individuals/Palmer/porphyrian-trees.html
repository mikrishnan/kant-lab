Arbor Porphyriana · A Scholarly Catalogue

The Tree of Porphyry

A hierarchical diagram of genus, species, and differentia — from Neoplatonism through the Scholastics to the modern logicians

Late Antique Early Medieval High Medieval Late Medieval Renaissance Early Modern Modern

The Arbor Porphyriana — the Tree of Porphyry — is a logical diagram first articulated in the Isagoge (c. 268 AD), the Neoplatonist philosopher's introduction to Aristotle's Categories. It arranges the five predicables (genus, species, differentia, proprium, accidens) into a branching hierarchy descending from the most universal substance to the individual.

Transmitted through Boethius's Latin translation and commentary, the tree became the central schema of scholastic logic. What follows is a catalogue of its principal treatments, arranged chronologically.

Late Antique & Patristic Period, 3rd – 6th century

Porphyry of Tyre c. 234 – c. 305 AD

Isagoge (Introduction to Aristotle's Categories)

The founding text. Porphyry introduces the five predicables and sketches the vertical hierarchy from Substance down through corporeal body, animate body, animal, rational animal, and finally the individual — Socrates, Plato. The tree is described in prose; it is later readers who render it as a diagram.

Neoplatonism Greek Founding text
Boethius c. 477 – 524 AD

In Isagogen Porphyrii Commentaria (1st and 2nd Commentaries); In Categorias Aristotelis

The crucial Latin transmission. Boethius translated the Isagoge twice and wrote two commentaries. His rendering of Porphyry's hierarchy into Latin — genus summum, genus subalternum, species specialissima — established the vocabulary that all medieval Latins would use. His prose describes what subsequent scribes would draw as the tree diagram.

Latin transmission Commentary Foundational vocabulary

Early Medieval Period, 8th – 10th century

Alcuin of York c. 735 – 804

Dialectica (dedicated to Charlemagne)

Written as a pedagogical dialogue for the Carolingian court school, Alcuin's Dialectica includes an exposition of the predicables drawn from Boethius. His treatment adapts the hierarchy for encyclopaedic instruction, making the tree schema accessible to students with no knowledge of Greek. An important conduit for the Porphyrian framework in the early medieval curriculum.

Carolingian Pedagogical Dialogue form
John Scottus Eriugena c. 815 – c. 877

Periphyseon (De Divisione Naturae)

Eriugena's encyclopaedic work fuses Neoplatonic hierarchy with Christian theology, deploying a grand division of Nature that mirrors and extends the Porphyrian schema. His fourfold division — Nature that creates and is not created; that is created and creates; that is created and does not create; that neither creates nor is created — represents the most ambitious theological transformation of the tree structure in the early medieval period.

Neoplatonism Theology Irish

High Scholastic Period, 11th – 13th century

Peter Abelard 1079 – 1142

Logica 'Ingredientibus'; Dialectica; Glosses on Porphyry

Abelard's commentary on the Isagoge is among the most incisive logical analyses of the tree in medieval thought. In his glosses he argues the famous nominalist position: universals such as "animal" or "body" are not things (res) but spoken words (voces) or mental concepts (sermones). His treatment of the tree thus becomes the central locus for the problem of universals in the 12th century.

Nominalism Universals debate Paris schools
Albert the Great (Albertus Magnus) c. 1200 – 1280

Liber de Praedicabilibus; Commentary on Porphyry's Isagoge

Albert's commentary on the Isagoge forms the opening part of his vast logical paraphrase of Aristotle. He expounds the tree with Aristotelian realism, arguing that universals have a foundation in real things (fundamentum in re). His treatment synthesises Avicenna's interpretation with the Aristotelian tradition, giving the tree a metaphysical grounding compatible with Dominican theology.

Dominican Aristotelianism Avicennan influence
Thomas Aquinas 1225 – 1274

In Librum Boethii De Trinitate; Commentarium in Porphyrii Isagogen (attributed)

Aquinas engages the tree principally through his metaphysics of essence and existence. In his commentary on Boethius's De Trinitate and in the Summa Theologiae, he deploys the genus-species hierarchy to distinguish modes of predication in theology. His moderate realism — universals exist formally in the intellect but have a foundation in the real nature of things — represents the canonical scholastic settlement of the problem the tree poses.

Thomism Moderate realism Dominican

Late Medieval Period, 13th – 15th century

William of Ockham c. 1287 – 1347

Expositio in Librum Porphyrii; Summa Logicae, Part I

also: Ordinatio (Commentary on the Sentences)

Ockham's engagement with the tree is radically deflationary. His nominalism holds that only individual things exist; genus and species are mental concepts (conceptus mentis), not entities in the world. The Porphyrian hierarchy therefore does not map a real ontological structure. In the Summa Logicae he subjects each predicable to detailed logical analysis while systematically dismantling their claim to extramental reality — the most sustained and influential critique of the tree's metaphysical ambitions.

Nominalism Via moderna Franciscan
Peter of Spain (Pope John XXI) c. 1215 – 1277

Summulae Logicales (Tractatus)

The most widely used logic textbook of the late medieval period, reprinted in hundreds of editions through the 16th century. Peter's Summulae opens with a treatment of the Porphyrian predicables before proceeding to the properties of terms. Its clear, schematic exposition of genus, species, and differentia became the standard pedagogical presentation across university faculties in Europe, carrying the tree into countless classrooms.

Textbook University curriculum Widely printed

Renaissance & Humanist Period, 15th – 16th century

Rudolf Agricola 1444 – 1485

De Inventione Dialectica

Agricola's influential humanist reformulation of dialectic reorients logic toward rhetoric and invention. He engages the Porphyrian framework critically, arguing that the scholastic preoccupation with the tree's metaphysical status distracts from logic's practical purpose — finding arguments. His work marks the beginning of the humanist re-evaluation of the tree as a tool for inventio rather than a map of being, influencing Ramus and the whole tradition of topical logic.

Humanism Dialectic Dutch
Petrus Ramus (Pierre de la Ramée) 1515 – 1572

Dialecticae Institutiones; Aristotelicae Animadversiones; Dialectique (1555)

Ramus transformed the Porphyrian framework into a visual technology for organising all knowledge. His method of dichotomous division — splitting every topic into paired branches — extended and radicalised the tree into the famous "Ramist charts": tabular diagrams printed as double-page spreads in Renaissance textbooks. Though departing from Porphyry's specific hierarchy, Ramism is the most historically consequential transformation of the tree's visual and epistemological logic.

Ramism Dichotomy Print culture

Early Modern Period, 17th – 18th century

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz 1646 – 1716

Dissertatio de Arte Combinatoria (1666); Nouveaux Essais; Correspondence with Arnauld

Leibniz saw in the Porphyrian hierarchy the seed of a universal calculus of concepts (characteristica universalis). In the early Dissertatio he applies combinatorial mathematics to the predicables, treating genera and species as composites of simpler conceptual atoms. His ambitious project anticipates both Boolean algebra and the semantics of natural kinds. The tree, for Leibniz, was not a scholastic relic but a formal structure awaiting algebraic treatment.

Combinatorics Universal language German rationalism
Antoine Arnauld & Pierre Nicole 1612–1694 & 1625–1695

La Logique, ou l'Art de penser (Port-Royal Logic, 1662)

The most influential logic textbook of the 17th century, written at Port-Royal and reprinted for two centuries. The Port-Royal Logic includes a clear and critical discussion of the Porphyrian tree in Part I, treating genus and species within a Cartesian framework that subordinates scholastic logic to the clarity of ideas. The authors retain the tree's pedagogical value while stripping it of metaphysical ambition — a distinctly modern, pragmatic appropriation.

Cartesianism Port-Royal French

Modern & Contemporary Period, 19th – 21st century

W. V. O. Quine 1908 – 2000

"Natural Kinds" (1969); Word and Object (1960); From a Logical Point of View (1953)

Quine's analytic philosophy engages the Porphyrian problematic directly in "Natural Kinds," asking what grounds the projectible predicates that form natural groupings. His critique of Aristotelian essentialism and his eventual pragmatic vindication of natural kind terms via reflective equilibrium represents the sharpest 20th-century confrontation with the metaphysical questions the tree raises, shorn of scholastic vocabulary but philosophically continuous with it.

Analytic philosophy Natural kinds Nominalism
OWL / Description Logics community 1990s – present

Web Ontology Language (OWL); Description Logic (ALC and extensions); W3C standards

The Porphyrian tree has a direct descendant in the class hierarchies of modern ontology languages. OWL's subClassOf relation formalises the species-genus link; the distinction between defined and primitive classes echoes differentia. The description logic tradition — Baader, Calvanese, McGuinness, Nardi, Patel-Schneider — has given the tree its most rigorous formal treatment, making it computable and embedding it in the infrastructure of the semantic web.
