Arbor Porphyriana

Trees of Genus and Species in Medieval Pedagogy

A catalogue of Porphyrian trees — diagrammatic representations of Aristotelian categories derived from Porphyry's Isagoge — as they appear across medieval logical and grammatical treatises from the ninth to the fifteenth century.

Select a Tree

On the Porphyrian Tree

Porphyry of Tyre (c. 234–305 ad) opened his Isagoge — an introduction to Aristotle's Categories — with a discussion of five predicables: genus, species, differentia, proprium, and accidens. Medieval commentators, translating and expanding this work, gave the logical relationships a visual form: a branching diagram descending from the most general genus (substantia) through successive differentiations to the lowest species and finally to the individual.

These diagrams, known as arbores Porphyrianae, became standard equipment in medieval logic instruction. They appear in manuscript margins, in diagrams prefacing school texts, and as elaborate illustrations in encyclopaedic works. Each tradition adapted the tree differently — varying the root, branch structure, labelling conventions, and the extent of the hierarchy.

This catalogue presents twelve trees drawn from pedagogical contexts spanning the Carolingian schools to the late medieval universities, with reconstruction diagrams and contextual notes.

Boethius — In Isagogen Porphyrii

Author: Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius  ·  Date: c. 504–510  ·  Language: Latin

Context

Boethius produced two Latin commentaries on Porphyry's Isagoge — a simpler one based on Marius Victorinus's translation and a more elaborate one based on his own — along with translations that formed the backbone of medieval logical instruction. His discussions of genus–species hierarchy established the visual and conceptual template for virtually all later Porphyrian trees.

Structure of this tree

The Boethian tree descends from substantia as the summum genus, bifurcating through differentia into corporeal and incorporeal substance, then through successive oppositions to the lowest species, homo. Individuals (Socrates, Plato, Callias) appear below the species line, outside the hierarchy proper.

"Genus est quod praedicatur de pluribus differentibus specie in eo quod quid est."

Predicables in use

  • Genus Substantia, Corpus, Animal
  • Species Homo (infima species)
  • Differentia corporalis/incorporalis, rationale/irrationale
  • Proprium risibile (capacity to laugh)
  • Accidens albedo, sessio

Transmission

Boethius's commentary survived in numerous Carolingian and later copies and served as the standard school text on Porphyry. The tree structure he described was reproduced in manuscripts of the Bamberg, Monte Cassino, and Tours traditions.

Neoplatonic Aristotelian logic summum genus infima species

Alcuin — Dialectica

Author: Alcuin of York  ·  Date: c. 790  ·  Context: Carolingian court school, Aachen

Context

Alcuin compiled his Dialectica as an instructional dialogue at Charlemagne's court. Presented as a conversation between teacher and pupil — or between Charlemagne and Alcuin — it is a simplified and Christianized adaptation of Boethian logic for courtly and monastic audiences with no prior philosophical training.

Pedagogical adaptation

Alcuin's tree is notably truncated: he compresses the Boethian hierarchy, removes the finer distinctions, and introduces a didactic framing that emphasizes the theological resonance of the summum genus. The tree becomes a mnemonic scaffold as much as a logical instrument.

"Genus summum est substantia, quae de omnibus praedicatur."

Significance

This treatise was widely copied in the ninth century and shaped how the Porphyrian tree was understood in Carolingian monasteries. It is an early example of the tree being adapted for non-specialist teaching, prioritizing clarity over completeness.

Eriugena — De Divisione Naturae

Author: John Scottus Eriugena  ·  Date: c. 864–866  ·  Language: Latin

Context

Eriugena's monumental Periphyseon (On the Division of Nature) adapts the Porphyrian genus–species hierarchy to a Neoplatonic Christian cosmology. Rather than a straightforward classification of substances, his fourfold division of natura describes the procession of all things from God and their return to the divine principle.

Theological inflection

For Eriugena, the tree does not merely classify: it narrates a cosmic movement. The categories are dynamic — the summum genus is not substance but natura itself, divided by the presence or absence of the ability to create and to be created. The Porphyrian schema is absorbed into a scheme of emanation and return.

"Dividimus itaque Naturam in eam quae creat et non creatur, et in eam quae et creatur et creat…"

Significance

This represents the most radical reinterpretation of the Porphyrian tree in the medieval period — preserving the logical form while inverting its ontological orientation. The diagram here is a reconstruction based on the fourfold division described in Book I.

Garlandus Compotista — Dialectica

Author: Garlandus Compotista  ·  Date: early 11th century  ·  School: Cathedral school, probably Liège

Context

Garlandus represents an early and radical position in the universals controversy: genera and species are mere words (voces), not real things or concepts. His Dialectica is one of the earliest surviving texts of what scholars call nominalism, predating Abelard's more nuanced position.

Nominalist modification

For Garlandus, the tree is a linguistic structure, not a map of reality. The nodes represent significant vocal sounds grouped by predication rules, not ontological categories. This produces a superficially traditional tree whose philosophical underpinning is quite different from the Boethian original.

"Genus est vox de pluribus differentibus specie in eo quod quid est praedicabilis."

Significance

Garlandus's Dialectica was rediscovered in the twentieth century and has since been recognised as a pivotal document in early medieval logic. His tree shows that the same visual schema could carry radically different theoretical commitments.

nominalism voces universals controversy cathedral school

Anselm of Canterbury — De grammatico

Author: Anselm of Canterbury  ·  Date: c. 1080–1085  ·  Language: Latin

Context

Anselm's De grammatico is not primarily a logical treatise but a philosophical dialogue using the problematic word grammaticus (one who is lettered) as a test case for understanding how paronyms — words derived from quality-terms — relate to the Porphyrian categories.

The grammaticus problem

The central puzzle: grammaticus appears to name a man (substance) but derives from grammatica (a quality, literacy). Where does it sit in the tree? Anselm's dialogue carefully distinguishes what a word signifies per se from what it signifies per aliud, with important consequences for how the tree is to be read.

"Grammaticus et per se significat qualitatem et per aliud substantiam."

Significance

Anselm's text was widely read in the twelfth century and influenced later discussions of signification and supposition theory. It shows the Porphyrian tree being used not just as a classificatory scheme but as a tool for probing the philosophy of language.

Peter Abelard — Logica Ingredientibus

Author: Peter Abelard  ·  Date: c. 1115–1120  ·  Language: Latin

Context

Abelard's Logica Ingredientibus is his major commentary on the Porphyrian predicables and the central text of his position in the universals debate. Against both the extreme realism of William of Champeaux and the crude nominalism of Roscelin and Garlandus, Abelard developed what he called the theory of sermones.

The theory of sermones

For Abelard, universals are neither mere sounds (voces) nor real things (res) existing apart from individuals. They are sermones — meaningful linguistic acts that express a shared intellectual conception. The tree thus represents communal ways of understanding, grounded in resemblance among individuals.

"Genus vel species est sermo de pluribus differentibus specie praedicabilis non quasi vox sed quasi sermo, id est intellectus communis."

Significance

Abelard's position became enormously influential. His Logica Ingredientibus, though never formally adopted as a school text, circulated widely and shaped twelfth-century debates on signification and predication. His tree is conceptually richer than those of his predecessors.

Gilbert of Poitiers — Commentary on Boethius

Author: Gilbert of Poitiers (Gilbertus Porretanus)  ·  Date: c. 1130–1148  ·  Language: Latin

Context

Gilbert of Poitiers, bishop and master of the cathedral school of Chartres, produced influential commentaries on Boethius's theological tractates. His distinctive ontological framework — the distinction between id quod est (that which is) and quo est or forma nativa (that by which it is) — represents a sophisticated realist position in the universals debate.

Forma nativa

For Gilbert, genera and species are not just words or mental concepts but are grounded in real formae nativae — native forms inhering in individuals that are structurally similar across them. This bridges the gap between Platonic realism and Abelard's conceptualism while maintaining the ontological grounding of the tree.

"Formae nativae sunt rerum proprietates quae de pluribus praedicantur."

Significance

Gilbert's commentary was condemned at the Council of Reims (1148), in part because his ontological distinctions were suspected of dividing the divine attributes. His school influenced twelfth-century realism at Paris and was studied by John of Salisbury, who left important descriptions of his teaching.

John of Salisbury — Metalogicon

Author: John of Salisbury  ·  Date: 1159  ·  Context: Dedicated to Thomas Becket

Context

John of Salisbury's Metalogicon is the most important twelfth-century account of the Porphyrian tree in its pedagogical and philosophical contexts. John studied under Abelard, Thierry of Chartres, Gilbert of Poitiers, and others, and his work provides a unique comparative survey of the approaches to universals he encountered.

Moderate realism

John himself favoured a moderate position — following his teacher Bernard of Chartres — that genera and species are concepts (intelligentiae) abstracted from individual things. The tree is a teaching device whose ontological commitments should not be pressed too hard. He famously called the extreme realist view a "monster in the nature of things."

"Siquidem universalia nusquam sunt nisi in singularibus, et ex singularibus trahuntur."

Historical value

The Metalogicon preserves descriptions of lost works and teaching styles from the cathedral schools of the early twelfth century, making it essential for the history of medieval logic and philosophy. Book II contains the most sustained engagement with the Porphyrian predicables.

moderate realism historical survey Bernard of Chartres intelligentiae

Thomas Aquinas — In Librum Boethii de Trinitate / In Porphyrium

Author: Thomas Aquinas  ·  Date: c. 1268–1272  ·  Language: Latin

Context

Aquinas engaged with Porphyrian logic throughout his philosophical and theological works, but most directly in his In Porphyrium and in discussions of being, essence, and predication in works like De Ente et Essentia. His treatment integrates Aristotelian logic with a robust metaphysics of essence and existence.

Metaphysical grounding

For Aquinas, the tree is grounded in real naturae communes — common natures that are in themselves neither universal nor particular but become universal when abstracted by intellect. This moderate realism, drawing on Avicenna's insight about the indifference of the nature, allows univocal predication across individuals.

"Natura communis secundum se considerata abstrahit ab omni esse, tam particulari quam universali."

Significance

Aquinas's treatment became the standard position in Thomist schools and, via them, in much later scholastic education. His commentary on the Isagoge served as the basis for countless later academic treatments of the predicables.

Thomism natura communis esse / essentia Avicenna

Ramon Lull — Arbor Scientiae

Author: Ramon Lull  ·  Date: 1295, Rome  ·  Language: Latin and Catalan versions

Context

Ramon Lull's Arbor Scientiae (Tree of Knowledge) is a monumental encyclopaedia organized entirely through the metaphor of trees. Rather than one Porphyrian tree, Lull constructs sixteen trees — the elemental tree, the vegetative tree, the human tree, the moral tree, the apostolic tree, and more — each representing a domain of knowledge with roots, trunk, branches, leaves, flowers, and fruit.

The Lullian transformation

Lull radically transforms the Porphyrian schema: instead of descending from summum genus to individuals, his trees ascend through ontological and moral levels, connecting the created order to God. The tree metaphor becomes both a mnemonic system and a missionary tool — Lull designed it partly to demonstrate the truths of Christianity to Muslims and Jews through universally accessible rational structure.

"Sicut arbor habet radices, truncum, ramos, folia, flores et fructus, ita haec scientia…"

Significance

The Arbor Scientiae had enormous influence, was translated into Catalan and French, and spawned a tradition of arboreal encyclopaedism that continued into the Renaissance. It represents the most ambitious extension of the Porphyrian tree metaphor in the medieval period.

William of Ockham — Summa Logicae

Author: William of Ockham  ·  Date: c. 1323  ·  Language: Latin

Context

Ockham's Summa Logicae is the most systematic presentation of nominalism in the medieval period. In Part I, he treats the Porphyrian predicables in detail, arguing that universals are mental signs (termini mentales) or conventional spoken or written signs — not things existing in the world outside the mind.

The razor and the tree

For Ockham, there are no real genera or species: only individual substances and qualities exist. The Porphyrian tree is a system of mental and linguistic signs that track objective similarities among individuals, but it does not reflect any real universal nature. The razor — do not multiply entities beyond necessity — cuts away the ontological commitments of earlier realists.

"Universale non est aliquid existens extra animam realiter in individuis nec in aliqua re."

Significance

Ockham's via moderna became enormously influential in fourteenth- and fifteenth-century universities, particularly at Paris and Oxford. His treatment of the Porphyrian predicables shaped later nominalist and conceptualist positions, and his work was on the curriculum at many institutions well into the sixteenth century.

Jean Buridan — Summulae de Dialectica

Author: Jean Buridan  ·  Date: c. 1335  ·  Language: Latin  ·  Context: University of Paris

Context

Buridan's Summulae de Dialectica is a comprehensive revision of Peter of Spain's thirteenth-century logic textbook, updated to reflect fourteenth-century developments in supposition theory and inference. Buridan was rector of the University of Paris and one of the most influential logicians of the late medieval period.

Supposition theory and the tree

Buridan brings the highly developed theory of supposition (the capacity of a term to stand for something in a sentence) to bear on the Porphyrian predicables. The question of what term like "animal" or "homo" stands for in different propositional contexts — whether for individual animals, for the species, or for the spoken word itself — is carefully distinguished.

"Terminus potest supponere personaliter, simpliciter vel materialiter, et secundum hoc variantur praedicationes in arbore."

Significance

Buridan's logic text was one of the most widely adopted in the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, rivalling Peter of Spain's Tractatus. His treatment of the predicables became standard in many central European universities. His famous thought experiment of the ass — uncertain between two equidistant bales of hay — is unrelated to the Porphyrian tree but shares the same systematic clarity of mind.
