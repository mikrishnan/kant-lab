What the records contain

Records come in four kinds, shown in the Status column:

  • Reported in the literature: a published source (scholarship, a library or museum image, a bibliographic description) places a tree in a specific edition, often with a page. The Evidence field names that source. Check it against a copy before relying on it.
  • Locus unchecked: the work is known to set out the arbor, but the edition and page still need checking.
  • Candidate: a major textbook worth examining; nothing is yet known about a tree in it.
  • Verified against copy: you have checked it yourself. No starter record has this status.

Tree transcriptions are only entered where a source gives the terms. Square brackets mark readings that are supplied or uncertain, and [?] marks a position not yet read. The demo record is a standard Latin skeleton, not taken from any book.

Writing a tree

One line per level, from the most general genus downward: the term, the retained differentia, the opposed differentia, and optionally the species the opposed branch names, separated by |. A line with no | is a term with no division at that level. A line starting with = lists individuals, separated by commas. Lines starting with # are comments. Keep the book's own spelling, abbreviations and case.

Substantia | corporea | incorporea | Spiritus
Corpus | animatum | inanimatum
Corpus animatum | sensibile | insensibile
Animal | rationale | irrationale
Homo
= Socrates, Plato

Secondary literature used

  • Paul Richard Blum, "Dio e gli individui: L'Arbor Porphyriana nei secoli XVII e XVIII", Rivista di filosofia neo-scolastica 91 (1999), 18–49. The main survey for the 17th and 18th centuries; discusses Pourchot.
  • Sylvain Auroux, "Dossier 5. Port-Royal et l'arbre de Porphyre", Archives et documents de la SHESL, 2nd ser., 6 (1992), 109–122. A dossier of texts.
  • Ludger Jansen, "Die Ontologie des Geschlechts" (Ruhr-Universität Bochum), which reproduces and describes Pourchot's tree.
  • Wilhelm Risse, Die Logik der Neuzeit, vol. II, 1640–1780 (Stuttgart-Bad Cannstatt, 1970). Cited by Auroux; a good finding aid for textbooks.
