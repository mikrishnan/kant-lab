What the percentages measure, and what they are good for
In one sentence
The percentage on a passage says how much of the same distinctive vocabulary it uses as the reference passage (the one framed in red): 0% means the two have no telling word in common, 100% would mean they use exactly the same words in the same proportions. It is a count of words, not a judgement about ideas.
How it is worked out
  1. Collect the words of each passage, and leave out two kinds: small words that carry no content (der, und, ist, nicht, also…), and the concept's own word. Every key passage on Wahrheit contains "Wahrheit", so counting it would make them all look alike.
  2. Even out the spelling, so that Urtheil and Urteil, Obiect and Object, Merckmahl and Merkmal count as one word, and trim the endings, so that Erkenntniß and Erkenntnißes do too. The text on screen is never changed; this is only for counting.
  3. Weigh each word by how rare it is across all three lectures. A word the lecturer uses everywhere (Erkenntniß, Verstand) counts for little, since sharing it says nothing; a word he uses seldom (Übereinstimmung, Object, Kriterium) counts for a lot. The words of the passage's definition-like sentence (shaded yellow) count three times, so that what weighs most is how each passage says what the concept is.
  4. Compare the two weighted word lists. The more of their heavy words two passages share, and the more of their weight lies in those shared words, the higher the score (cosine similarity, shown ×100).
On the passages now on screen
How to read the numbers
  • Real passages seldom go above about 40%, because even close parallels differ in most of their words. Roughly: 20% and up means the same formulation recurs; 10–20% some shared terms; under 5% next to nothing in common. The bars are full at 50%.
  • Compare the numbers with each other, for the same reference: which passage in each lecture is closest, and by how much. Change the reference and every number changes.
  • Long passages score lower simply because they contain more that the reference lacks.
What it is good for
  • Finding the parallel passage. The three transcripts are notes from the same course on the same textbook, years apart, so the lecturer often comes back to the same point. The highest score in each lecture is the best place to look for where he did.
  • Seeing whether a formulation was kept or replaced. Two passages that both define the concept, one scoring high and one low against the same reference, are a pointer that the definition was worded differently in one of them. That is often where a change of view shows, though it is not proof of one.
What it cannot tell you
  • Whether two passages agree. The same thought in other words scores low: synonyms are not matched, nor Latin with German (conceptus and Begriff). Opposite claims in the same words score high, since nicht is one of the words left out.
  • What a passage means. A low score says "worded differently", which is a reason to read both passages side by side, not a finding about Kant. The finding is yours to make from the texts.
