      Reading with a Porphyrian tree

      In his Isagoge (3rd century), Porphyry showed how a highest genus, substance, divides by opposed differences (corporeal or incorporeal, animate or inanimate, rational or irrational) until it reaches a species like human being. Every species is then defined by its proximate genus plus its specific difference.

      Kant thinks this way constantly. He builds his books as divisions, and in places he draws the ladder himself: the division of representations at A320/B376 is a textbook Porphyrian tree. Each diagram here maps one such division. Pick a node and the panel shows its genus and difference, the branches it rules out, Kant’s location in the text, a gloss, a question to keep in mind while reading, and links to the same idea in other trees.

      Where Kant’s text resists the form, the tree says so. Dashed nodes are either empty classes (like the analytic a posteriori) or formulations that are aspects of one principle rather than species of a genus. Noticing where a division breaks down is part of reading Kant well.

      Citations

      The Critique of Pure Reason is cited by the pages of the first (A, 1781) and second (B, 1787) editions; other works by volume and page of the Academy edition, so 4:421 is volume 4, page 421. These margins appear in most modern translations. Page references mark where a section begins or where a point is made, and are worth checking against your own edition.
