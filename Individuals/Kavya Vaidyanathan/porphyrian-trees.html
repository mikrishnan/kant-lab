c. 268 CE

Isagoge (Introduction to Aristotle's Categories)

Porphyry of Tyre (c. 234–c. 305 CE)

The founding textual source of all subsequent Porphyrian trees. Written while Porphyry studied under Plotinus in Rome, the Isagoge sets out the five predicables — genus, species, difference, property, and accident — in a hierarchical schema descending from the supreme genus of Substance. No diagram appeared in the original text; the tree existed purely as logical description.

Greek Neoplatonism Textual only

c. 505–510 CE

Commentary on the Isagoge of Porphyry

Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (c. 477–524 CE)

Boethius produced two Latin translations and commentaries on the Isagoge, the second vastly more elaborate. He is credited with first visualising the scala praedicamentalis as a diagram — likely a simple ladder or branching schema — embedding it in the standard logic curriculum of medieval Europe. His version became the foundational logic textbook for nearly seven centuries.

c. 870–880 CE

Arbor Porphyrii in the Categoriae Decem

Attributed to Pseudo-Augustine (anonymous, 4th–9th c.)

Manuscript copies of De decem categoriae — a Carolingian-era paraphrase once attributed to Augustine — frequently included marginal branching diagrams closely related to the Porphyrian schema. Paris, BnF lat. 12949 preserves one such tree beginning with Usia (the Latinised Greek for Substance), with Cicero as the exemplary individual.

Latin Manuscript Carolingian

c. 1220–1240

Introductiones in Logicam

William of Sherwood (c. 1200–c. 1272)

Among the earliest scholastic texts to incorporate and name 'Porphyry's Tree' explicitly as a pedagogical device, Sherwood's Introduction to Logic helped standardise the diagram within the emerging university curriculum. His tree descends from Substance through Body, Animate, Sensate, Rational, and Man, with Socrates as the terminal individual.

Latin Scholastic University

c. 1230–1245

Summulae Logicales (Tractatus)

Peter of Spain (Petrus Hispanus) (c. 1205–1277)

The most widely circulated logic textbook of the Middle Ages, surviving in nearly 400 manuscript copies. Peter of Spain gave the diagram its canonical Latin name Arbor Porphyrii and was among the first to depict it as a true, leafy tree — complete with vegetative ornament — rather than a schematic ladder. His version placed Plato and Socrates as examples of homo at the tree's root.

c. 1240–1260

Logica (Dialectica) — Porphyrian Tree

John of Damascus / manuscript at Paris, BnF MS lat. 16598 (13th-century copy)

A thirteenth-century manuscript of John of Damascus's Logica, donated to the Sorbonne by master Gérard d'Abbeville, incorporates a theologically inflected Porphyrian tree in which Peter and Paul replace Plato and Socrates as the exemplary individuals of the species Man — a telling adaptation for a text read by theologians.

Latin Theological Sorbonne

1295

Arbor Scientiae (Tree of Knowledge)

Ramon Llull (c. 1232–c. 1316)

Llull's ambitious encyclopaedic work extended the Porphyrian structure far beyond logic into theology, natural philosophy, law, medicine, and the arts — generating sixteen distinct trees, each structured by the same binary-division method. His Arbor naturalis et logicalis places a version of the Porphyrian tree at the centre, flanked by ten types of questions (right) and ten logical keys (left), making it the most elaborate medieval transformation of the schema.

1475

Loyca parva (LJS 457, fol. 7v)

Paolo Veneto (c. 1370–1428), copied at Perugia

This manuscript of Paolo Veneto's Loyca parva, written in 1475 by a scribe at the abbey of the Hermits of Saint Augustine in Perugia, preserves one of the more refined late-medieval illustrated Porphyrian trees. Now held at the University of Pennsylvania Libraries (LJS 457), it demonstrates the continued vitality of the diagrammatic tradition well into the quattrocento.

Latin Manuscript Pennsylvania

1512

Logica Nova — Arbre des relations logiques

Ramon Llull (posthumous edition)

A 1512 printed edition of Llull's logical works preserves an elaborate tree of logical relations derived from the Porphyrian schema. Printed in folio format, the diagram demonstrates how the tradition migrated from manuscript illumination into woodcut printing with relatively little loss of diagrammatic complexity.

Printed Woodcut Lullism

c. 1550–1572

Ramist Trees (Dialectique)

Petrus Ramus (Pierre de la Ramée) (1515–1572)

Ramus adapted the Porphyrian dichotomous method into what came to be called 'Ramist trees' — simplified, single-axis bifurcations stripped of the ontological depth of the original. Applied to grammar, rhetoric, and all branches of learning, Ramist diagrams prioritised visual clarity over metaphysical precision and exerted enormous influence on Protestant educational practice across northern Europe.

French/Latin Reformed Pedagogy

c. 1718–1724

Deckenfresko, Bibliothekssaal Kloster Schussenried

Franz Georg Hermann (1692–1768)

An extraordinary painted ceiling fresco in the library hall of Kloster Schussenried in Baden-Württemberg incorporates a Porphyrian tree as part of a sweeping allegorical programme of knowledge. Hermann's baroque treatment embeds the logical schema within figural painting, demonstrating how the diagrammatic tradition had by the early 18th century become iconographic — a symbol of learning as much as a working tool.

German Fresco Baroque

1730

Arbor Porphyrii — Institutiones Philosophicae, Vol. I

Edmond Purchot (Purchotius) (1651–1734)

Purchot's Institutiones Philosophicae, a Cartesian-inflected philosophy textbook widely used in French Jesuit colleges, includes a finely engraved Porphyrian tree. Printed in Paris, it is one of the last major formal presentations of the schema in an active pedagogical context — and one of three trees (alongside those of Boethius and Llull) most commonly reproduced in modern scholarship as representative of the tradition's full arc.
